# Motivation

Suppose you are given a set of experts and have to choose a sequence of decisions, where in each decision you must choose an expert to follow.
After every decision, you learn how all of the experts performed. Your goal is to do as well as possible,
and in particular at the end of all of the decisions you would like to have performed almost as well as the best expert in hindsight.
Using the multiplicative weight updates algorithm, you can get a good bound on the gap between your performance and the best expert.

In many areas of complexity theory, we find that reductions between different problems and models enable
novel approaches and results. The problem which the multiplicative weights algorithm addresses is one which
many scenarios can be reduced to, some more obviously than others.

One example is to find approximately optimal strategies in zero-sum games.
The primary question here is "What can you guarantee even when your opponent knows your strategy?"

We will conclude by showing how randomized lower bounds can be proved in communication complexity using multiplicative weight updates.
Here the question of interest is the relation between public-coin randomized communication complexity and distributional communication complexity.

# Prerequisites

Learning the multiplicative weights algorithm requires primarily a willingness to deal precisely with algebra, logic, and basic probability.
A background in algorithms and online algorithms in particular may help provide intuition and make the content easier to understand. 

The regret lower bound deals more heavily with probability, but probability is not essential to understand the intuition behind it.

Prior experience with 2 player games is not necessary to understand the ways in which this algorithm applies. 
Matrix-vector multiplication and transposes are used for 2 player games.

For the application in communication complexity, prior knowledge of protocols, distributional communication, and randomized communication is necessary.
A brief reference can be found at [Communication Complexity and Applications](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~toni/Courses/CommComplexity2022/Lectures/randomized-cc.pdf). 
This reference covers the theorem we prove, but we will suggest a different proof.
