# Unreliable Experts

Consider the following situation. You are given a sequence of $T$ conjectures, one by one.
After being given conjecture $t$, you must respond with whether you think it is true or false. If you are correct, you get a point.
Unfortunately, you have lost your intuition, but fortunately there is a set of $N$ experts, each of which provides their opinion on the decision.
Before deciding, you can see if each of these experts thinks it is true.
After deciding, you can see how all of the experts performed.
The goal is to make as few mistakes as possible.

To gain some intuition for how we might hope to optimize this, try the following games.

In the following game, 2 of the experts are always correct, the other 8 are completely random.

In the next game, each expert has an accuracy drawn from the normal distribution.

In the first setting, it is clear that a reasonable strategy is to go with the majority of currently flawless experts.
Every time the majority of flawless experts is wrong, at most half of the previously flawless experts were correct, which means the set of flawless experts shrinks exponentially
with the number of mistakes the algorithm makes. So as long as there is one flawless expert, the number of mistakes is logarithmic in $N$.
But it is very rare that there are perfect experts, and if all experts are flawed then this algorithm will eventually be left with no flawless expert to follow.

It is much less clear what the right approach to the second game is.
First, we must precisely determine what our goal should be.
If all of the experts are random coin flips, we should not expect our algorithm to do better than a coin flip either.
But if all of the experts are mosly correct, then we want our algorithm to also be mostly correct.
We will capture this with the goal of minimizing regret.
We will also generalize the situation to involve arbitrary decisions instead of boolean ones, 
so the error of a decision will be captured by a loss function that takes values in $[0, 1]$.

# The Problem

Let $T$ be the number of decisions and $N$ be the number of experts as before. 

Let $ell_t : [N] -> [0, 1]$ be the loss function of each expert for decision $t$. 

On decision $t$, we may use any of the loss functions $ell_r$ for $r < t$ to determine our strategy.
In some cases we may want to use randomness, such as for the first decision if all of the experts are split evenly.

Let $p_t : [N] -> [0, 1], sum_(i = 1)^N p_t (i) = 1$ be the probability distribution our strategy chooses to determine which expert we follow for decision $t$.
Then the loss of our strategy on decision $t$ is $p_t dot ell_t$, which is the expected value of our loss if we choose an expert by our probability distribution.

Formally, we seek to design an algorithm to minimize the following, which we refer to as the regret of our algorithm.
```typst
$ sum_(t = 1)^T p_t dot ell_t - min_(i in [N]) sum_(t = 1)^T ell_t (i) $
```
The term we are subtracting is the minimum total cost of all $T$ decisions of a single expert, which is what our cost would be if we could
see the future and then were forced to choose exactly 1 expert to follow for all $T$ decisions.
In other words, this would be our minimum cost if our strategy was to pick 1 expert and stay with them the whole time,
and then we got very lucky and selected the expert who minimizes the cost of this strategy.

Here are a few more examples of situations which we hope to design an algorithm for.
The first is detailed formally and the other two will be detailed in later pages.

<DisclosureBlock id="weather-forecasting">
  <span slot="summary">Weather Forecasting</span>

Suppose each day there are the same $N$ weather forecasters predicting certain likelihoods of rain between $0$ and $1$.
Assuming news source $n$ says the probability of rain is $q_n$ on day $t$, the loss will be $ell_t (n) = 1 - q_n$ if it rains, $ell_t (n) = q_n$ otherwise.

Each day t, you must select a probability distribution $p_t$ which will be used to decide which news source you follow.
Your loss will be the same as the loss of the news source you follow.
But in expectation, your loss will be $p_t dot ell_t$.

How should you choose your probability distribution each day so that your cumulative expected loss stays close to the best fixed forecaster in hindsight?
</DisclosureBlock>

<DisclosureBlock id="dodgeball">
  <span slot="summary">Dodgeball</span>

Suppose you are playing dodgeball with your friend, and you intend to play many times.
You can either throw the ball right at him, at his left, or at his right.
You then see where your friend dodges and can use this information to help determine future play.
This problem can be modeled as an instance of choosing between these 3 decisions with a loss of 1 if you miss.
How should your strategy evolve over time to win as much as possible?
</DisclosureBlock>

<DisclosureBlock id="network-flow">
  <span slot="summary">Bandwidth Distribution</span>

A streaming provider needs to stream the finals of the World Cup to hundreds of cities.
Each city needs a certain amount of bandwidth.
The provider can split a city's traffic across several routes, but routes to different cities can share the same fiber link.
How should the provider route the traffic so every city gets enough and the busiest link has as much spare capacity as possible?
</DisclosureBlock>

In the following page we will detail the multiplicative weights algorithm, which performs well in all of these situations.
Not only does it perform well, but in the most general setting it is asymptotically optimal, in that for any online algorithm,
there exists a sequence of losses on which it performs asymptotically at least as bad as multiplicative weights' worst case regret.
